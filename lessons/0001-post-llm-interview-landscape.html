What Changed: Interviewing in the LLM Era

Before drilling any algorithm, calibrate the target. You're rusty on interview mechanics, not on engineering — the biggest risk right now isn't "forgot how a hash map works," it's preparing for an interview format that no longer exists. This lesson fixes that.

Why this comes first

Your mission explicitly calls out understanding the post-LLM interview landscape as its own thread — not a footnote. That's the right instinct: if you spend eight weeks grinding under 2019 assumptions and the actual room has changed, you'll waste prep time and get caught off guard on interview day. So: knowledge first, then drills.

The headline split

As of 2026, Google and Amazon have landed in different places on AI-assistant use during interviews. Google is piloting a Gemini-assisted "code comprehension" round for some junior/mid roles — not yet universal, and not the default. Amazon's stance is stricter: AI use detected during an interview has been reported as grounds for disqualification.

Two independent trackers looking across the industry broadly agree: roughly 64% of companies still ban AI-assistant use in interviews outright. [1] The safe default, walking into any of these three companies, is to assume unaided unless a recruiter tells you otherwise in writing.

Recall — no peeking
Without scrolling up: which of the three target companies is reported to disqualify candidates for AI use, and which is piloting an AI-assisted round? Say it out loud before continuing.

The numbers behind "it's changed"

The largest dataset found on this (19,368 interviews analyzed) shows just how mainstream AI use in interviews has become — and how interviewers are actually responding to it:

  • 38.5% of interviews were flagged for suspected AI use.
  • 48% of those flags were in technical (coding) rounds specifically.
  • 61% of flagged candidates still passed the interview.

[2] That last number is the one worth sitting with.

What's the most defensible read of "61% of flagged candidates still passed"?

What's actually being tested now

Regardless of a company's written policy, practitioners converged on the same observation: interviewers have adapted by pushing harder on live, unaided explanation — walk through your approach, defend your trade-offs, debug on the spot, answer "why not X instead" without a pause. [3] This is the actual skill under evaluation now — solving the problem was always necessary, but it is no longer sufficient by itself.

Practical implication
When we get to coding drills, we won't just practice reaching a working solution. We'll practice narrating it out loud, under time pressure, as if someone is watching — because that's the actual bar now, independent of any single company's AI policy.

Take-homes are shrinking

Take-home assignments have become one of the easiest formats to complete with AI assistance undetected, and companies have responded by de-emphasizing them or pairing them with a mandatory live follow-up where you explain and modify your own submission on the spot. If a take-home shows up in your loop, expect that follow-up — prepare for it as part of the take-home, not as a surprise.

What this means for your prep plan

  • Assume no AI assistance in any live round, at any of the three companies, unless a recruiter says otherwise in writing.
  • Practice explaining and defending solutions out loud — this becomes a standing element of every future coding lesson in this workspace, not a one-off drill.
  • Don't skip pattern fluency because "AI can generate the code" — the bar has moved to live comprehension, which requires you to actually know the pattern, not just recognize the output.
  • If a take-home appears in your loop, treat the live explanation follow-up as part of the assignment.
